No Hot Water? What To Check Before You Call A Plumber

What's safe to check yourself, and the real signs it's time to call, without the scare tactics.

Have This Issue Now?
(703) 912-0567
Call Now

No hot water is one of those problems that feels like an emergency the moment it happens, and sometimes it genuinely is, but sometimes it's a two-minute fix you can handle yourself before picking up the phone. Here's an honest breakdown of what's actually worth checking, and what isn't safe to mess with.

Safe to Check Yourself

Is it actually the water heater, or the whole house? If no fixture in the house has hot water, that points to the water heater itself. If only one fixture lacks hot water while others work fine, the water heater is probably fine, and the issue is isolated to that fixture's own valve or mixing cartridge.

Check the breaker, for electric units. A tripped breaker is a common, genuinely easy fix that gets mistaken for a failed water heater. Check your electrical panel before assuming the worst.

Check the pilot light, for older gas units. Many gas water heaters have a visible pilot light, and if it's gone out, most units have a simple relight procedure printed right on the tank. If you're not comfortable with this or the unit doesn't have a straightforward pilot, that's a reasonable point to stop and call rather than push further.

Check the thermostat setting. It sounds obvious, but a thermostat accidentally bumped down, sometimes by a curious kid or during other work near the unit, is a real, embarrassingly simple cause we've found more than once.

Signs It's Genuinely Time to Call

  • You smell gas near the unit. Stop immediately, don't attempt to relight anything, leave the area, and call from a safe distance. This is the one item on this list where waiting or investigating further isn't worth the risk.
  • Visible water pooling around the base. This can mean a loose fitting, which is a simple fix, or a failing tank, which isn't, and telling the difference isn't something to guess at.
  • Rust-colored water. This points to internal tank corrosion, which a repair can't reverse, and continuing to use the unit doesn't make the underlying issue better.
  • The pilot won't stay lit after multiple attempts. This usually points to a thermocouple issue, a component-level problem rather than something a relight procedure fixes.
  • The unit is more than 10-12 years old. At this age, a no-hot-water situation is more likely to be a sign of broader tank failure than an isolated, cheap fix.

Why "Just Wait and See" Sometimes Backfires

A water heater that's intermittently failing, working fine for a day, then no hot water again, then fine again, often gets waited out by homeowners hoping it resolves on its own.

In our experience, this pattern usually indicates a component that's failing gradually rather than a stable, resolved issue, and catching it during the intermittent phase, before it fails completely, usually means a smaller repair instead of an emergency replacement on the coldest morning of the year.

Tank Vs. Tankless: Does Troubleshooting Differ?

Tankless units are actually more diagnostic-friendly in one specific way: most display a specific error code when something's wrong, which points directly to the likely cause before a technician even arrives.

If you have a tankless unit and it's showing an error code, that code is worth noting and mentioning when you call, it often means a faster, more targeted repair. Any repair or replacement work, tank or tankless, gets completed to Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) standards for venting and connections, the same standard we follow on every water heater job.

What We Actually Check First

When we get a no-hot-water call, we inspect the tank, connections, and venting before touching anything, then diagnose based on what we actually find rather than assuming based on the unit's age alone. If a repair genuinely makes sense, we'll do that.

If the unit is old enough that a repair is throwing money at a system near the end of its life anyway, we'll say so honestly, with the real numbers for both options, rather than defaulting to whichever is more profitable for us.

The Bottom Line

Check the breaker, check the thermostat, and if it's a gas unit with an accessible pilot, check that. If none of that resolves it, or you smell gas, see rust-colored water, or notice pooling at the base, that's when a call makes sense, sooner rather than after a few more cold showers.

What If Only Some Fixtures Have Reduced Hot Water?

This is a different problem from a total loss of hot water, and it points somewhere else entirely. If one shower runs out of hot water fast while another fixture seems fine, that often points to a mixing valve or a fixture-specific issue rather than the water heater itself.

If hot water seems generally weaker throughout the house but never runs out completely, that can point to sediment buildup reducing the tank's effective capacity, a genuine sign it's time for a flush or, if it's been neglected for years, a sign the tank's efficiency has already declined enough to warrant a broader conversation about its remaining lifespan.

Preventing This From Happening Again

An annual flush, draining the tank to clear out sediment buildup, is one of the more overlooked maintenance tasks that genuinely extends a water heater's working life, particularly on this area's moderately hard water supply where mineral buildup is a real, ongoing factor.

It's a task most homeowners never think to schedule until something already feels off, but doing it proactively, before symptoms show up, is a meaningfully cheaper habit than waiting for a no-hot-water call.

Keeping a Simple Record Helps More Than You'd Think

If your water heater has had more than one issue over the past couple of years, jotting down roughly when each one happened and what it turned out to be is genuinely useful information, both for spotting a pattern and for giving whoever diagnoses the next issue real context instead of starting from zero.

A unit that's had three unrelated minor issues in two years is telling you something different than one that's had zero issues in eight years and just hit a rough patch. That context changes whether a repair or a replacement conversation makes more sense the next time something goes wrong.

When a Second Opinion Is Worth Getting

If you've been told your water heater needs full replacement and something about that recommendation doesn't sit right, particularly if the unit is relatively young or the diagnosis happened quickly without much explanation, a second opinion is a reasonable thing to ask for.

A legitimate diagnosis should be able to explain specifically what's failing and why repair isn't a viable option, not just point to the unit's age as the sole justification. We'll always walk you through the actual reasoning, not just the recommendation, so you can make an informed decision either way.
